ABSTRACT 
Retail buyers now expect to browse, compare and pay for goods from a phone or laptop at any hour. 
Small and mid -sized sellers, however, still depend on walk -in counters, telephone orders and 
spreadsheets, which leads to stock mismatches, lost orders, slow fulfilment and no consolidated 
view of sales. 
This case study documents the analysis and design of the Online Shopping System (OSS) , a role -
based web platform in which Customers browse and purchase products, Vendors list items and fulfil 
orders, and Administrators govern users, promotions and reporting. All requirements are derived from the 
approved Software Requirements Specification (SRS v1.0), which defines 35 functional requirements 
across eight feature areas and nine non -functional requirements covering performance, security and 
availability. 
The report selects the Incremental process model , justifies it against alternatives, and assesses 
technical, economic, operational and schedule feasibility. The system is designed as a three-tier 
architecture: a responsive single -page front end, a stateless RESTful API (Node.js/Express with JWT -
based RBAC) and a MongoDB document database. Analysis and design are communicated through use 
case, class, sequence, activity, data -flow and entity -relationship diagrams, together with collection 
schemas and key algorithms such as stock-safe checkout and coupon validation. 
A BlueCart interface prototype of 26 screens, covering the customer storefront, vendor console and 
administrator panel, was designed to validate the requirements visually. A test suite of 30 designed test 
cases is traceable to every feature area. The report closes with advantages, limitations and a roadmap 
for future releases. 
Keywords: Online Shopping System, E -commerce, SRS, Incremental Model, UML, Role -Based Access 
Control, JWT, MongoDB, Shopping Cart, Payment Gateway, Prototype.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Overview 
Shopping has gradually moved from physical bazaars to digital storefronts. A modern customer expects 
to search a catalogue, read honest reviews, compare prices, apply a discount code, pay securely and 
track the parcel, all without speaking to anyone. Behind this simple experience sits a tightly coordinated 
set of services: a product catalogue, an inventory ledger, a cart, a payment link, an order pipeline and a 
reporting layer. 
Smaller retailers rarely have such infrastructure. Orders arrive through phone calls and chat messages, 
stock is tracked on paper or in a spreadsheet, and the owner learns what sold well only at the end of the 
month. The Online Shopping System (OSS)  studied in this report is designed to close that gap. It is a 
centralised, role-based web application that manages the whole life cycle of an online retail operation, 
from listing a product to delivering it and analysing the sale. 
The study is based on the approved SRS (Version 1.0, July 2026), which follows the IEEE Std 830 -1998 
structure. The SRS was converted into design artefacts, a test plan and a visual prototype, so that the 
same requirement can be followed from the document to a diagram, to a test case and finally to a 
screen. 
1.2 Problem Statement 
Retail businesses that still rely on manual or loosely connected tools face four recurring problems:  
• Inventory mismatch:  the same item can be promised to two buyers because stock is updated by 
hand, which leads to cancellations and unhappy customers. 
• Slow, error-prone ordering: phone or in-person orders need a human to note down items, calculate 
totals and confirm payment, so mistakes in price and quantity are common.  
• Fragmented responsibilities:  vendors, store staff and owners use different notebooks or files, so 
nobody has one authoritative view of products, orders and customers. 
• No decision support:  without structured sales and stock data, restocking and promotion decisions 
are taken on instinct rather than evidence. 
 
1.3 Objectives 
• Replace phone and counter ordering with a self-service storefront  where customers can browse, 
search, filter, manage a cart and pay online. 
• Give vendors a console to list products, maintain stock and fulfil orders  for their own catalogue 
only. 
• Give administrators one consolidated panel for users, vendors, coupons, reviews and sales reports. 
• Guarantee data integrity and secure access  through JWT authentication, bcrypt hashing, role -
based access control and PCI-DSS-compliant payment delegation. 
• Keep the design scalable and portable through a stateless API tier and a containerisable back end. 
 
1.4 Scope of the Project 
In scope (Version 1.0): 
• Three role-based workspaces: Customer storefront, Vendor console and Administrator panel.  
• Registration, login, password reset, account lockout and role-based authorisation. 
• Catalogue, SKU generation, stock control, search, filtering, ratings and reviews.  
• Cart, wishlist, coupons, checkout, gateway payment, order tracking, cancellation and returns.  
• Email/SMS notifications, sales and low-stock reports with CSV export. 
 
Out of scope (candidates for later releases): 
• Point-of-sale integration for physical stores and barcode scanners. 
• Third-party marketplace syndication and logistics or fleet management.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW & BACKGROUND 
2.1 Existing Systems 
• Manual and phone-based ordering: the buyer calls or visits, the seller writes the order in a register 
and confirms stock from memory. There is no audit trail and no way to scale beyond a few dozen 
orders a day. 
• Spreadsheet and chat -app selling: catalogues are shared as images or sheets and payments are 
collected through personal transfers. Records are scattered across devices, and a single overwritten 
cell can corrupt stock figures. 
• Hosted marketplace listing:  sellers rely on a large third -party marketplace. Reach is high, but 
commissions are heavy, the seller does not own the customer relationship, and branding, promotions 
and data access are limited by the marketplace. 
• Generic site builders:  template-based builders offer a storefront quickly but offer limited control 
over role-based workflows, custom reports or vendor-specific permissions. 
 
2.2 Limitations of Existing Systems 
• No real-time stock validation, so overselling is possible during busy periods. 
• No unified cart-to-delivery workflow; payment, packing and tracking happen in separate tools.  
• Weak access control: staff often share one login, so actions cannot be traced to a person.  
• Reports are produced by hand and arrive too late to guide restocking or discount decisions.  
• Sensitive payment details may be handled informally, which is a compliance and trust risk.  
 
2.3 Proposed System: BlueCart OSS 
The proposed OSS is a single, self -contained web product that supports three roles under one login 
system. The cart and checkout logic runs on the server, so a price or quantity cannot be altered from the 
browser. Stock is decremented only when payment succeeds, and every administrative action is written 
to an immutable audit trail. Payment cards are never stored; the gateway handles all card data.

3.4 Business Rules 
• Only an Administrator may create or deactivate vendor accounts. 
• A Vendor may manage only products and orders attached to their own catalogue.  
• A Customer can only view a confirmed order, apart from requesting cancellation or a return.  
• Confirmed orders are immutable except through a logged Administrator or Vendor override.  
• Deletions are soft deletes with a 30-day recovery window; transaction records are kept for 7 years.

CHAPTER 5: SYSTEM DESIGN 
5.1 System Architecture (Three-Tier Topology) 
The OSS uses a decoupled three -tier architecture. The browser renders the interface and talks only to 
the REST API; the API owns all business rules and is the only component allowed to reach the database 
or an external service. 
 
Figure 5.1: Three-tier architecture of the OSS with external services 
The presentation tier hosts three role-specific workspaces in one responsive single-page application. The 
application tier is a stateless Node.js/Express service whose modules mirror the SRS feature areas. The 
data tier holds eight MongoDB collections. The payment and email gateways are reached only from the 
application tier, so card data never touches the platform's own servers.

• Routes map HTTP endpoints to controllers and return consistent 400/401/403/404 errors.  
• Middleware performs JWT verification, role checks, request validation and input sanitisation before

• Services hold business logic: stock reservation, coupon validation, order state transitions and report 
aggregation. 
• Models (Mongoose schemas) define fields, unique indexes and references to other collections.

6.4 Key Algorithms 
6.4.1 Login with account lockout (FR-02, FR-07) 
function login(email, password): 
    user = users.findByEmail(email) 
    if user is null: return error 401 
    if user.lockUntil > now(): return error 423 "Account locked" 
    if bcrypt.compare(password, user.passwordHash): 
        user.failedAttempts = 0 
        return jwt.sign({id: user.id, role: user.role}, SECRET, expiresIn 24h) 
    user.failedAttempts += 1 
    if user.failedAttempts >= 5: user.lockUntil = now() + 15 minutes 
    return error 401 
 
6.4.2 Stock-safe checkout (FR-11, FR-17, FR-19, FR-20) 
function checkout(userId, addressId, shippingMethod): 
    cart = carts.find(userId) 
    for line in cart.items:                       # step 1: validate 
        if products.stock(line.sku) < line.qty: return error "Insufficient stock" 
    total = pricing.total(cart, shippingMethod)   # server-side price 
    session = gateway.createSession(total)        # step 2: payment 
    wait for webhook(session) 
    if webhook.status == "paid":                  # step 3: atomic commit 
        begin transaction 
            for line in cart.items: products.decrement(line.sku, line.qty) 
            order = orders.create(cart, address, total, status="Confirmed") 
            carts.clear(userId) 
        commit 
        notify.email(userId, order) 
        return order 
 
6.4.3 Coupon validation (FR-16, FR-29) and rating update (FR-26) 
function applyCoupon(cart, code): 
    c = coupons.find(code) 
    if c is null or now() < c.validFrom or now() > c.validTo: return error 
    if c.usedCount >= c.usageLimit: return error "Coupon exhausted" 
    discount = (c.type == "percent") ? cart.subtotal * c.value / 100 : c.value 
    return min(discount, cart.subtotal) 
  
function updateRating(sku, newRating):            # new average of n+1 ratings 
    p = products.find(sku) 
    p.avgRating = (p.avgRating * p.reviewCount + newRating) / (p.reviewCount + 1) 
    p.reviewCount += 1

CHAPTER 9: ADVANTAGES, LIMITATIONS, FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS & 
CONCLUSION 
9.1 Advantages 
• One platform serves customers, vendors and administrators with strictly separated permissions.  
• Server-side pricing and stock validation prevent tampering and overselling. 
• Sensitive data is protected through bcrypt, TLS, input sanitisation and a PCI-DSS gateway. 
• A stateless API and containerised back end allow horizontal scaling for sale events.  
• Reports and CSV export give owners evidence for restocking and promotion decisions.  
 
9.2 Limitations 
• Version 1.0 supports one currency and the English language only. 
• Shipment tracking is limited to vendor-updated statuses; live courier integration is future work. 
• The prototype is a visual design; payment and notification flows need real gateway credentials to be 
exercised end to end. 
• No multi-vendor commission or settlement logic is included (TBD-02). 
 
9.3 Future Enhancements 
• Courier API integration for real-time shipment tracking (TBD-05). 
• Multi-currency and multi-language support (TBD-04). 
• Vendor commission and settlement engine for a full multi-vendor marketplace. 
• Recommendation engine and personalised home page based on browsing and purchase history.  
• Mobile applications and WebSocket-based real-time order notifications. 
 
9.4 Conclusion 
This case study converted the Online Shopping System SRS into a complete engineering package: 
justified process model, feasibility analysis, architecture, UML and database designs, algorithms, a 
traceable test plan and a 26 -screen interface prototype. The Incremental model fits the prioritised 
requirements, and the three -tier, stateless design satisfies the performance, security and scalability 
goals. The result gives a clear and testable blueprint from which the four planned increments can be 
implemented.
